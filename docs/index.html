Origins & formative networks

The Foro de São Paulo grew out of the Latin American left of the 1980s — socialist and communist parties, trade unions, guerrilla movements turning to electoral politics, and Christian currents. In Brazil, the Workers' Party (PT), the Forum's convener, was itself built from a coalition of trade unionists, Marxists and Catholics of the Liberation Theology tradition (the Christian base communities, CEBs).

Liberation Theology as a formative current

Liberation Theology — the Latin American Catholic movement linking the Gospel to social and political liberation — was one of the streams feeding the PT and the broader regional left that later convened the Forum. Clerics such as the Dominican friar Frei Betto and the theologian Leonardo Boff bridged the Catholic left, the PT, and revolutionary governments such as Cuba's.[16][17]

Frei Betto — bridge to Cuba ?

Frei Betto (Carlos Alberto Libânio Christo), a Dominican friar and Liberation Theology figure, was a spiritual adviser to Lula and held long interviews with Fidel Castro that became the 1985 book 'Fidel and Religion' (Fidel e a Religião). He is widely credited in Brazilian accounts with brokering Lula's relationship with Fidel — the specific 'introduction' is reported in those accounts rather than independently documented here.[17]

1985 Havana debt conference — an early Lula–Fidel encounter

Lula first travelled to Cuba in 1985 for a Havana conference convened by Cuba on the Latin American foreign-debt crisis, with broad political, religious and social participation — five years before the Forum's 1990 founding. It is among the early contacts between the Brazilian left and Havana that prefigured the Forum's networks.[18]

Armed & guerrilla movements in the Forum

The Forum was founded as a gathering of the whole Latin American left, and its participants included not only electoral parties but organizations that had waged, or still waged, armed struggle. Some — the FSLN in Nicaragua, the FMLN in El Salvador, the URNG in Guatemala, the MLN-Tupamaros in Uruguay — were guerrilla movements that became legal parties (the first two are listed among the parties above). Others took part as active armed organizations, most notably Colombia’s FARC and Chile’s MIR. Several of these groups were formally designated terrorist organizations by governments including the United States and the European Union; those designations are noted below with attribution. The precise extent and formality of each armed group’s participation in specific Forum meetings is documented unevenly, so those points are marked to verify and, where contested, attributed rather than asserted.
A claim in circulation holds that “the FARC elected a president.” This conflates two distinct groups. The FARC’s successor party, Comunes, holds seats guaranteed by the 2016 peace accord in Colombia’s Congress but has never won the presidency. Colombia’s president elected in 2022, Gustavo Petro, is a former member of a different, earlier-demobilized guerrilla movement — the M-19 — and was the first former guerrilla to win the Colombian presidency.

Fuerzas Armadas Revolucionarias de Colombia — Ejército del Pueblo (FARC-EP) ?

Colombia · 1964–2017 (armed); legal party since 2017[20][21][22]

Nature: The hemisphere’s longest-running Marxist–Leninist guerrilla insurgency; during the conflict it financed itself partly through kidnapping-for-ransom and the taxation of the cocaine trade.

Designation: Designated a Foreign Terrorist Organization by the United States (1997) and listed as a terrorist organization by the European Union; treated as such by successive Colombian governments during the armed conflict.

Role in the Forum: Took part in Foro de São Paulo encuentros during the 1990s and 2000s, when the Forum brought together armed and electoral currents of the left.

Status today: Signed the 2016 peace accord and demobilized; in 2017 became a legal party — the Fuerza Alternativa Revolucionaria del Común, which kept the “FARC” acronym — renamed Comunes in 2021. The accord guarantees it 10 congressional seats for the 2018–2026 terms. It has not won the presidency.

Movimiento de Izquierda Revolucionaria (MIR) ?

Chile · 1965–1990s[25]

Nature: Revolutionary-socialist movement that took up armed struggle, especially in clandestine resistance to the Pinochet dictatorship (1973–1990).

Designation: Repressed as a subversive organization by the Chilean military regime; many of its members were killed, disappeared or exiled.

Role in the Forum: Took part in early Foro de São Paulo meetings.

Status today: Effectively defunct as an armed force after the return to democracy; only small successor groupings remain.

Movimiento 19 de Abril (M-19) ?

Colombia · 1970–1990[24][23]

Nature: Nationalist, left-wing urban guerrilla, known for the 1980 Dominican embassy siege and the 1985 storming of the Palace of Justice in Bogotá.

Designation: Treated as a terrorist/subversive organization by the Colombian state during its armed period.

Role in the Forum: Demobilized in 1990 — the year the Forum was founded — and continued in legal politics through the Alianza Democrática M-19.

Status today: Demobilized 1990. Former members stayed active in politics; Gustavo Petro, elected President of Colombia in 2022, is a former M-19 member.

Movimiento de Liberación Nacional–Tupamaros (MLN-T) ?

Uruguay · 1960s–1970s (armed); legal since the 1980s[26]

Nature: Urban guerrilla movement crushed under the 1973–1985 dictatorship, its members imprisoned for years.

Designation: Prosecuted as a subversive/terrorist organization under the Uruguayan dictatorship.

Role in the Forum: Joined the Frente Amplio — a founding-era Forum member — and takes part through it.

Status today: Legal; part of the Frente Amplio coalition. José Mujica, a former Tupamaro imprisoned during the dictatorship, was President of Uruguay (2010–2015).

Unidad Revolucionaria Nacional Guatemalteca (URNG) ?

Guatemala · 1982–1996 (guerrilla coalition); legal party since 1998[27]

Nature: Coalition of Guatemalan guerrilla organizations during the civil war (1960–1996).

Designation: Treated as an insurgent/terrorist coalition by the Guatemalan state during the war.

Role in the Forum: Signed the 1996 peace accords and became a legal party; participant in the Forum.

Status today: Legal political party (small).

Member parties in government

Parties that belong to or are aligned with the Foro de São Paulo have held the presidency across much of Latin America — a measure of the network's electoral weight. (The region has also elected non-FSP, right-leaning governments, so this is a pattern, not exclusive control.) Founding/membership status of some parties is still being verified (see parties list).

Related organizations

The Foro de São Paulo is often confused with newer left/progressive networks. It has not been renamed — these are distinct, coexisting organizations that sometimes coordinate or meet alongside it.

Grupo de Puebla (Puebla Group)

July 2019 · Puebla, Mexico

Composition: Individual progressive leaders — presidents, ex-presidents, ministers, parliamentarians and intellectuals — rather than political parties.

Relation to the Foro: A distinct, complementary organization — NOT a renaming of the Foro de São Paulo. Launched in 2019 with the help of Foro-aligned networks; it notably does not include parties from Venezuela, Cuba and Bolivia, partly to distance itself from the authoritarian-left image. Both organizations remain active and have met side by side, e.g. in Tegucigalpa, June 2024.

https://www.grupodepuebla.org/

Progressive International

2020 · Global

Composition: Global network of left/progressive movements, parties and figures.

Relation to the Foro: Allied international network; co-present with the Foro and the Puebla Group at the June 2024 Tegucigalpa gatherings. Separate organization with a worldwide (not Latin America–specific) scope.

https://progressive.international/

CELAC Social

2010s · Latin America and the Caribbean

Composition: Social-movements space associated with the Community of Latin American and Caribbean States (CELAC), an intergovernmental body.

Relation to the Foro: Allied civil-society space, distinct from the Foro (which is a party forum, not an intergovernmental one). Co-present at the June 2024 Tegucigalpa gatherings.

Analyses & perspectives

Several authors and outlets analyze the Foro de São Paulo as a coordinating network of the Latin American left, tracking the connections between its member parties and the spread of left governments in the region. These works are influential in Brazil's political debate. Some specific claims (e.g. formal ties to armed groups, or direct control of governments) are debated; such claims are attributed to their authors rather than asserted here as established fact.

O Foro de São Paulo: A ascensão do comunismo latino-americano

Olavo de Carvalho · 2022 · Vide Editorial (posthumous compilation)

A long-time analyst of the FSP, Carvalho argued it functions as a coordinated project advancing the Latin American left, and traced links between its member parties and movements — including, in his account, armed groups such as the FARC and Chile's MIR. He was central to bringing the FSP into Brazilian public debate; the strongest of these claims are debated. Much of this analysis was published through his outlet Mídia Sem Máscara (2002–2014, since revived), which also circulated a compiled “Atas do Foro de São Paulo”.

https://www.amazon.com/Foro-S%C3%A3o-Paulo-latino-americano-Portuguese/dp/8595071861

O Foro de São Paulo: A mais perigosa organização revolucionária das Américas

Graça Salgueiro · Rádio Vox — Observatório Latino

Argues the FSP is a far-reaching revolutionary network operating across the Americas, including through influence over media. Developed in her book and on her Rádio Vox program Observatório Latino.

https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/32619274-o-foro-de-s-o-paulo

Commentary on the FSP, the 'Latin American axis' and the New World Order
